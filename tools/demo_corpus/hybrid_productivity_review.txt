Does hybrid work change productivity? A review of 27 studies
C. Lindqvist, Centre for Labour Studies (fictional), Policy review, 2025

Overview. Studies of hybrid work report mixed effects on productivity. Of 27 studies, 11 found higher productivity, 9 found no difference and 7 found lower productivity.

Where productivity rose. Productivity rose most in jobs with long periods of concentrated individual work, such as programming and writing. Fewer interruptions at home were the main reason given.

Where productivity fell. Productivity fell in jobs that depend on quick coordination with colleagues, and among new employees who needed training.

Measurement. Most studies relied on self-reported productivity. Studies that measured output directly found smaller effects than studies that asked employees.

Conclusion. There is no evidence that hybrid work reduces productivity overall; the effect depends on the kind of work.

Selection of studies. We searched four databases for studies published between 2020 and 2024 that compared hybrid or remote workers with office workers on a measure of productivity. Twenty-seven studies met our criteria: twelve surveys, nine studies using company records, and six field experiments.

Experiments. The six field experiments, in which employers assigned home-working days by lottery, found on average no difference in output between home and office days. One experiment in a call centre found a small rise in calls handled at home, and one in a design firm found slower progress on team projects.

Hours worked. Several studies found that remote workers worked longer hours, partly in the evening. Higher output per week can therefore hide lower output per hour.

Implications for employers. Employers should decide about hybrid work task by task: home days for focused individual work, office days for training, coordination and creative team work.
