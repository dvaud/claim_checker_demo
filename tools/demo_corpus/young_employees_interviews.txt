"You never really meet anyone": young employees on remote work
Interview study, Department of Sociology, University of Easthaven (fictional), working paper, 2024

We interviewed 42 employees aged 21 to 29 who started their first job during or after the pandemic. Most worked from home three or more days a week.

Loneliness. Many respondents described feeling lonely and cut off. Several said that they had never met some of their colleagues in person. Respondents who lived alone felt this most strongly. A minority enjoyed the quiet and did not feel lonely at all.

Learning. Respondents said that remote work made it harder to learn the unwritten rules of the job. They missed overhearing how experienced colleagues handled problems.

Office days. Respondents valued office days when their team came in on the same day. Office days when nobody else was there were described as pointless.

We conclude that remote work increases loneliness among young employees who live alone, unless employers organise regular team days.

Method. Respondents were recruited through three employers (a bank, a software company and a government agency) and through social media. Interviews lasted about an hour and were coded thematically by two researchers.

Wellbeing. Respondents valued the flexibility of remote work and the time saved by not commuting. Several said they slept better and exercised more. However, those who lived alone said that whole weeks at home made them feel low and unmotivated.

Career worries. Many respondents worried that managers would not notice their work. Some came to the office more often than required for this reason, even though they found the office day tiring.

Recommendations. Employers who want young staff to thrive should plan regular days when whole teams meet, pair new employees with a mentor, and make sure that online meetings leave room for informal conversation.
