Always on? Working hours and home working: a survey of 2,312 union members Rivermouth Federation of Employees (fictional), member survey report, 2024

Background. The Rivermouth Federation of Employees (fictional) is a trade union for office, public-sector and service staff. Since 2021 its members have asked more and more often about working from home, and in spring 2024 the federation ran its first full member survey on the subject. This report presents the main results for members who work at a desk for at least part of the week.

Method. A questionnaire was sent to 9,800 members and 2,312 answered, a response rate of 24 percent. Respondents reported their contracted hours, the hours they actually worked in the last full week, and where they worked on each day. Answers were weighted by sector and age. Members who never work from home form the comparison group in everything that follows.

Hours. Members who work from home at least three days a week reported 3.4 more unpaid hours a week than members who work at the office every day. Most of the extra time falls in the evening, between eight and eleven o'clock. Members with one or two home days reported 1.1 more unpaid hours, a smaller gap. Hours were reported by the members themselves, not taken from employer records.

Availability. Almost half of the respondents who work from home (47 percent) said that they feel expected to answer messages outside working hours, compared with 22 percent of office-based respondents. Only one in five had a written agreement with their employer on when they can be reached. Members in small teams felt this pressure more often than members in large departments.

Boundaries. Members explained the longer hours mainly by the lack of a clear end to the working day: with the laptop on the kitchen table, closing it feels optional. Others said that they start earlier because there is no train to catch. The survey did not measure whether the extra hours produced extra output, and members were divided about whether they were worth it.

Stress. Among members who work from home three or more days a week, 38 percent described their stress as high, against 31 percent of office-based members. The difference disappeared among members who had a written agreement on availability: in that group 29 percent reported high stress. Members were asked about the last four weeks and could choose between low, medium and high.

Travel. Members who work from home three days a week saved on average 2.6 hours of travel a week. They valued this time, but 29 percent said that they spent most of it on additional work rather than on rest or family. Members who live more than an hour from the office saved the most. Members who travel by train valued the saving most.

Family. Members with caring responsibilities were more likely to say that working from home helps them to combine work and family: 71 percent of parents of children under twelve agreed, against 52 percent of other respondents. Women and men with children gave similar answers. Members without children more often said that home working made it harder to switch off.

Monitoring. Seventeen percent of respondents who work from home said that their employer uses software that records keystrokes or screen activity. Members who are monitored in this way were twice as likely to say that they feel distrusted by their manager. Many also said that the software encouraged them to look busy rather than to do the work well.

Office days. Members were divided about compulsory office days. Where a whole team came in on the same days, 64 percent called the office day useful; where attendance was left to individuals, only 33 percent did. Members who commute by train were the most critical of days fixed by the employer without notice. The question was answered by 1,980 members.

Recommendations. The federation asks employers to agree in writing when staff can be reached, to count hours worked at home in the same way as office hours, and to refrain from keystroke monitoring. It also asks that the right to work from home be set in collective agreements, not left to the judgement of individual managers.
