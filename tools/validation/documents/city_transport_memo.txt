Commuting patterns after the pandemic: memo to the Transport Committee Northbridge City Transport Office (fictional), 2026

Purpose. This memo summarises the travel data of the Northbridge City Transport Office (fictional) for 2019 to 2025 and asks the committee to decide on next year's timetable. It draws on ticket sales, ring road counters and the annual household travel survey, which covers about 3,000 households. Figures for 2025 are provisional.

Rail. Rail passengers into Northbridge on Fridays fell by 31 percent between 2019 and 2025, while passengers on Tuesdays and Wednesdays fell by only 6 percent. Season ticket sales fell by 27 percent as many commuters switched to flexible tickets. Monday travel lies between the two, with a fall of 17 percent. Weekend travel has recovered more fully than weekday travel.

Car traffic. Ring road counts at the morning peak are 2 percent above their 2019 level on Tuesdays to Thursdays and 12 percent below on Fridays. Freight and delivery vans account for most of the growth, which fits the national pattern. Counts on Mondays are 5 percent below the 2019 level. The counters sit on the ring road and do not cover the streets inside the city.

Distance. In the household survey, the average one-way commuting distance of employees who work from home at least one day a week rose from 17 to 19 kilometres between 2019 and 2025, because many of them moved to smaller towns outside the city. Employees who do not work from home show no change in distance. The survey asks about the usual route, not about every journey.

Emissions. The office estimates that transport emissions from commuting in the city were 9 percent lower in 2025 than in 2019. About two thirds of the fall is attributed to fewer trips and one third to the growing share of electric cars. Fewer trips follow mainly from the days that employees now spend at home. The estimate carries a margin of about two percentage points.

Peak crowding. On Tuesdays and Wednesdays, trains between half past seven and half past eight carried on average 112 percent of seated capacity, against 94 percent in 2019. Operators cannot lower costs on quiet days, since the fleet and the drivers are paid for the whole week. Fridays now run at about 41 percent of capacity at the same hour.

Active travel. The share of cycling in commuting trips rose from 14 to 19 percent, driven by the new cycle lanes opened in 2022. The household survey does not show whether home working changed how many people cycle. Walking trips to work were unchanged at 6 percent. Bicycle parking at the central station was extended in 2024.

Pilot. In September 2025 three employers with 5,200 employees began a trial in which office days are spread across the week by agreement. After six months, peak crowding on Tuesdays and Wednesdays fell from 112 to 103 percent of seated capacity, and Friday occupancy at the same hour rose from 41 to 48 percent. The employers report no complaints from staff.

Costs. The cost of the rail service run by the regional company rose by 8 percent between 2019 and 2025 because of higher wages and energy prices, while ticket revenue fell by 14 percent. These figures are the main reason why the committee is asked to reconsider the timetable. A decision is needed before the budget is set in March.

Recommendations. The office recommends adding two trains on Tuesday and Wednesday mornings, removing one on Friday afternoons, and extending the pilot to ten employers. It also recommends that the city ask employers to publish their office days so that the timetable can follow them. The committee is expected to vote on the proposal in January.

Limitations. The household survey is answered by about 1,100 working households each year, so differences of one or two percent are within the margin of error. Ticket data record travel by pass or card but not the reason for the journey, and the office cannot separate commuting from leisure travel with certainty. The office therefore treats small changes as noise.
